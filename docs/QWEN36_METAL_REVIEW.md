## 0. Summary

Three findings drive the rest of this review:

1. **The engine on the other side of the comparison is now identified exactly.** The Unsloth Desktop build is `b11724-mix-c208e5f`: upstream llama.cpp `b11724` plus 20 pinned commits (the release page enumerates every one). **No pinned commit touches Metal compute** — the compute-relevant pins are CUDA (MoE prefill #264, cpy fast path #157, MMVQ batch cap #287), HIP (#149, #158) and Vulkan (#270). On the M2, the benchmark ran **upstream ggml-metal kernels** with the model card's flags. That is good news for attribution and good news for the roadmap: the prefill bar we are chasing is the quality of upstream ggml-metal, and the fork's own CUDA work (#264) shows what "kernel structure" is worth even in that mature engine (+16.4–17.8% on Qwen3.6 prompt processing; decode +0.1%, i.e. tie).
2. **Two of the four Phase 2 initiatives survive review; one is redundant; one attacks a ceiling the 32k experiment proved is not there.** T9 (batched prefill attention) and T10 (batched MoE GEMM) are the right homework. T8 (prefix caching) is homework generated by misreading a fresh request — the feature shipped in T0 (`metal/src/engine/q36_metal.cpp` L2419–2429, documented in `docs/QWEN36_METAL.md` §2.2). T11 (paged context) aims at a wall that the 32,768-token run on the *same 24 GB machine* showed does not exist; it should be reframed from "break the ceiling" to "budget predictably at the ceiling". Two cheap measurement tickets are missing and should come first.
3. **The article's numbers that disagree with the recorded record are misquotes, not measurements.** The GDN-scan and dense-MMA speedup paragraphs quote three different value sets for the same two measurements; only the diagram's values match `docs/QWEN36_METAL.md` §10. Corrected numbers and sources are in Table A. The earlier walk-back commit (`b7806f1`, 2026-10-19) already fixed the MTP attribution and the "static KV table" framing; what remains is listed in §9.

The maintainer's three research questions get direct answers in §4: why Unsloth prefills faster (dispatch structure, not magic — and the deep-decode numbers show it is *not* falling off a cliff at depth, which is our T6 opening); what we have that they do not (measured, itemized); and whether a combined implementation is possible (yes, with the memory math to back it — the 32k experiment already proved the memory side on their engine).

## 3. New evidence since the article was written: the 32k experiment

All numbers in this section are from the maintainer's screenshots (Unsloth Desktop, model card telemetry page, 2026-10-19). They are user-collected evidence, not instrumented telemetry — treat them as strong observations that the reruns below should confirm.

**The sequence.** After the article's 8.2k truncation, the maintainer raised the context manually to 32.8k on the same 24 GB M2. With Chrome closed, the model loaded with one yellow memory warning and **no failure**. The first request measured prefill **259.9 tok/s** and decode **14.4 tok/s** — while the machine was under peak contention (CPU ~99%, RAM 94% Warning, swap grew 1.09 → 5.91 GB in about a minute, 204°F), and at only ~2.2k of context. A follow-up request (5,850 prompt-cache hits, ~447 fresh tokens at 136.0 tok/s, 5,020 generated) **recovered to 21.2 tok/s sustained from ~6.3k to ~11.3k depth, with Chrome open again**.

**What this separates:**

- **Refuted premise:** the 8.2k wall was never an architecture limit. 32k loads and runs on 24 GB on their engine; Strata's split-K + `--kv-f16` already sustain 16k+ on the same machine. T11's "break the ceiling" premise is gone (§5).
- **Depth-independence of prefill:** 259.9 tok/s at a 32k-window session vs 263.6 at 8k — prefill rate is set by kernel/batch structure, not by context depth. This is the cleanest possible confirmation that the prefill gap is a T9/T10 problem and the deep-context problem is a different lever (T6).
- **The confound:** 14.4 tok/s at ~2.2k depth, hot and swapping, is not a "decode at depth" number; it is a "machine under contention" number. It is *shallower* than the 8k run that measured 23.8. Nobody should publish either number as a depth effect.
- **The real story:** 21.2 tok/s sustained across 5,020 tokens from 6.3k→11.3k depth, warm machine, Chrome open — the strongest decode-at-depth number in either camp's record, and it sits at ~26–30% of the M2's bandwidth ceiling at that depth (arithmetic in §4.1). ggml-metal's deep decode does **not** fall off a cliff. That is the number Strata's T6 + `--kv-f16` path has to meet, and the projection says we can (§4.1, with the assumption labeled).
- **The comparison rule this teaches:** the follow-up's prefill read 136.0 tok/s on ~447 fresh tokens (3.29 s) with 5,850 cache hits — versus ~260 on 5.8k tokens. Prefill tok/s depends strongly on batch size; fixed per-request overheads amortize. **Every cross-engine prefill comparison must match batch sizes or state them.** (It also shows, from their side, the same prefix-cache behavior our T0 shipped — see row 4.)

**Before any 32k decode number is published:** a cool-machine rerun, Chrome closed, temperature noted in both states (204°F contended vs cool), decode tok/s sampled at matched positions (~2k, ~6k, ~11k) on both engines (§7, block 4).

## 4. The three research questions

### 4.1 What makes Unsloth's implementation faster than ours?

**Prefill: dispatch structure.** Their engine evaluates a prompt in whole-ubatch GEMM kernels — one dispatch per matrix per ubatch, flash-attention over the chunk, sorted-batch MoE. Strata's chunk path still dispatches attention per row (a deliberate T2 parity decision, `q36_metal.cpp` L1274–1278) and routes experts via gathered GEMV (`push_c_gemv`/`push_c_lpr8`). Strata's own T3 measurement explains why chunking alone did not close it: at depth, chunked and token-by-token read a 4,098-token prompt in 243 vs 245 s — the binding term is the KV re-read (every query head re-reads the same cells, ~8×), which the parity contract kept identical on both paths. So the gaps decompose cleanly:

- **Batch structure** (per-row attention + per-entry expert GEMV) → T9 then T10. The fork's pinned #264 proves the MoE part is worth real percentages even in the mature engine (+16.4–17.8% pp on Qwen3.6, decode tie) — and it is **CUDA-only**, so on Metal a good batched MoE GEMM is a differentiator, not catch-up.
- **Fixed per-dispatch overhead** → also attacked by fusing; the 136-vs-260 small-batch observation shows these overheads are real and material.
- **KV traffic at depth** → T6's GQA fold (built, awaiting its M2 measurement) plus `--kv-f16`.

**Decode at depth: nobody is falling off a cliff — and that defines our target.** Per output token at depth L the engine reads: weights ~0.97–1.16 GB (3.1B active at ~2.5–3.0 bpw), QSA KV (10 layers × 2 × 2 KV heads × 256 dim × 2 B = 20.5 KB per token of context; 232 MB at 11.3k), and the GDN recurrent state (~31 MB/token, depth-independent: 30 layers × 32 V-heads × 128² × 2 B). At 100 GB/s:

| Depth | Per-token reads | Bandwidth ceiling | Unsloth measured | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~0.4k | ~1.0–1.2 GB | ~83–99 tok/s | 31.2 tok/s | ~31–37% |
| ~11.3k | ~1.23–1.42 GB | ~70–81 tok/s | 21.2 tok/s | ~26–30% |

The same ~25–35% efficiency class at both depths — their deep decode is the same kernels absorbing the KV growth, no cliff. Strata's recorded all-levers deep numbers (12.2–16.3 tok/s client-side at ≤4k depth, fp32 KV, no fold — §10 ad-hoc) sit at ~14–22% of their ceiling at those depths. The fold cuts KV traffic ~16× (8× from the fold on 16/2 geometry × 2× from f16), which raises the 11.3k-depth ceiling to ~76–89 tok/s. **If** Strata's kernels then run at the same ~25–35% efficiency class ggml-metal demonstrates, the projection is **~19–31 tok/s at 11.3k depth — parity with their measured 21.2**. The assumption is labeled: whether the efficiency class carries over to the folded path is exactly what T6's pending A/B measures (today's 14–22% may reflect occupancy or dispatch costs that folding does not remove). This is why T6's measurement jumps to the front of the queue.

**What it is not (yet proven):** MTP (row 6 — two branches, one log line decides), and fork tuning (no Metal pins; the compute pins are CUDA/HIP/Vulkan). On the M2, we are comparing Strata against **upstream ggml-metal**.

### 4.2 What have we done that they haven't?

Measured or verified, item by item:

1. **Split-K attention past the 8k threadgroup-memory ceiling** with auto-engage above 8,192 cells (T1; 32K boots and generates — M2-verified).
2. **An explicit startup working-set refusal** with an override (`STRATA_Q36M_MEM_OK=1`) — the engine refuses to allocate what the machine does not have, instead of guessing and paging (T1 scope; their Auto *guesses*, conservatively, and the guess is the thing that truncated the article's Test 2).
3. **`--kv-f16`, measured** (§9): 0.34 → 0.17 GB at 8K, 1.34 → 0.67 GB at 32K. Honest inversion worth stating publicly: llama.cpp *already defaults to f16 KV*; our fp32 default exists to keep the bit-parity verify contract, so this flag is us catching up to the conventional default — the differentiator is the fold, not f16.
4. **MTP, implemented in-tree, with an honest measured negative verdict on the M2** (T4/T4.5): 5.0–11.1 tok/s vs the 17.5–21.4 base — the verify window (three rows through 40 layers at ~15–25% acceptance) dominates, so `--mtp` stays strictly opt-in. Two footnotes: (a) the card's "1.5–2× with MTP" is llama.cpp's `draft-mtp` at `-np 1` — a different cost structure (shared embeddings, draft-n-max sweep) our verdict did not measure; the calibration experiment in §7 block 5 is the named path to revisit, and the reviewer's expectation is that a calibrated win in the 1.5–2× range is plausible once the window cost is tuned; (b) the fork pins MTP support for qwen4exp (#240/#241) but not for the Qwen3.6 arch's trimmed sidecar.
5. **The parity culture itself.** 38-stage bit-parity harness, byte-identical defaults, measured error envelopes where reassociation is unavoidable (1e-3 for MMA, ~5e-4 fp16 operand rounding). The fork's own #194 shows the demand exists in their world too: an *opt-in* exact-concurrency mode making output byte-identical regardless of neighbours, at a measured cost where stated (~9–15% on solo decode in their tables; the 35B solo figure 15.6% predates their one-launch expert path and was not re-measured — their numbers, cited as such). **We ship that contract as the default.** For a sovereign-computing audience, reproducibility-by-default is the pitch, stated in the competitor's own terms.
6. **The WY parallel scan and the MPP matrix-unit GEMM donor** (§10: 14.6×/14.9× GDN, 15.3× dense, TTFT −40–44% on short chunks) — mechanisms they do not ship for this model.
7. **Measured deep-context generation** on hardware we own, with the confounds named (this review, §3).

### 4.3 Is a combined implementation possible?

**Yes.** The memory math on 24 GB: ~11.4 GB resident weights (UD-Q2_K_XL) + 0.67 GB KV (f16 at 32k, §9 measured) + scratch (~59 MB WY at chunk 512, ~236 MB at 2048, ~40–80 MB MMA arena) + app → **~12.5–13 GB working set, leaving ~10 GB** for Chrome, the IDE and macOS. The 32k experiment proved the memory side empirically on their engine; ours has the same shape plus the explicit refusal gate. The blockers to their prefill speed are kernel dispatch structure (T9/T10), not memory; the deep-context decode gap is T6's pending measurement against T11's ≥6 tok/s-at-16k gate. Nothing about the combined plan requires a bigger machine — it requires the measurements first (§6 order), then the two batch-structure kernels behind parity knobs.

## 6. What Phase 2 is missing (and the resulting order)

1. **Re-baseline first.** Every Phase 2 target is currently set against the stale ~25 tok/s bringup number. First ticket: the fresh all-levers prefill/decode matrix on the M2 (§7 block 1).
2. **Dispatch-count instrumentation.** A one-off counter for Metal dispatches per chunk (per path: per-row attention, MoE GEMV, chunked).
